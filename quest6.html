Quest 6 - The culture games

These is the comparison between our native countries of Iran, Moldova, Portugal, Switzerland and Ukraine versus the Netherlands according to the Geert Hofstede country comparison bar charts (https://geerthofstede.com/country-comparison-bar-charts).

Iranian differences - Individualism & KLong term orientation - Kourosh

Individualism

In comparison to the Netherlands which has highly shifted to individually behaving and free-thinking, the people of Iran have by far less of individualism in their way of life. The culture of Iran is highly oriented around the family, society and their values and expectations. Families have high expectations from their members, especially children and every individual must act according to their community and follow a set of instructions to be valid. I personally saw this several times, mostly when my schoolmates were choosing the major they wanted to continue their study in. Many of them were under the influence of their families and private consultants to choose exactly what their families wished them to become, regardless of their personal interests, talents and wishes about the future.

Long-term orientation

Because of several factors but most importantly the economic term, people are more likely to invest their money and time in short term plans and/or pleasures. In comparison to the Netherlands, where people seems to be more certain about their future (and schedule their plans for long run actions, because of which), Iranians are not certain about a bright future, thus they tend to make their lives as much as they can in present time instead of spreading it to years and more. Hopefully, I have not experienced this, personally, thanks to my lovely family and their great supports, but I know this as a fact, as shown in the diagram above.

Moldovan differences - Power distance & Individualism - Constantin

Power distance

Moldova has very high-Power Distance. Not addressing a teacher, boss, or superior person with the proper respect, merely a first name without including "Mr./Ms." and last name, is offensive. In The Netherlands, hierarchy is less strict, and workers and students can freely discuss things with professors or bosses.

Individualism

Although Moldova is shown as being collectivist on the chart people are self-centered. Even when they are working in groups, people argue over who works harder or is better. The Netherlands, on the other hand, represents individualism and personal achievement but with personal responsibility and equality in teams.

Portuguese differences - Power distance & Uncertainty avoidance - Leandro

Power distance

The level of Power Distance in Portugal is greater than it is in the Netherlands because hierarchy and authority are taken for granted and unquestioned. Individuals in Portugal will defer to teachers, managers, or older individuals a lot. Decisions are usually made by authorities, and disagreeing with authority will make someone feel uncomfortable. The Netherlands, on the other hand, assumes equality and open communication. In the Dutch culture, students call their teachers by their first names, express their opinions in class, and expect to be treated as equals. When I started taking classes here, I was puzzled why students had the audacity to disagree with students. I returned from a cultural background where I would feel awkward disagreeing with an instructor and here it is called situational respect. When collaborating, this difference means that the Dutch expect equal contributions from all parties, whereas the Portuguese may wait for the “leader” to make decisions on their behalf. Adapting to the Dutch method has made me more confident to share an opinion upfront.

Uncertainty avoidance

Portugal is extremely high in Uncertainty Avoidance, and people very much like to have rules, plans, and security. I constantly use to see people prepare for everything; for example, having written schedules, or enforcing strict rules in the classroom. Meanwhile, the Dutch are much more accommodating and flexible to change. I noticed that in Holland, people plan spontaneously, and are open to new ideas. When I first moved here, I was shocked by how laid back the Dutch are with time and organization. Meetings or group project sometimes begin late, or plans change last minute; this would be incredibly stressful in Portugal. I grown accustomed to this flexibility and have learned to embrace it as a strength; it focuses on creativity rather than being stressed out when things don’t go according to plan.

Swiss differences - Masculinity & Individualism - Niels

Masculinity

This was a honestly surprising metric to me. I do believe that the Swiss are somewhat more masculine, esp. in the non German speaking areas, but to me it does not feel that extreme. Also by living a bit in the least of the Netherlands, I also feel like that score is too low for that estimate. Both areas I’ve lived in were fairly farmers dominated, so that should not influence my feeling that much, and to me they felt very much comparable in fixations on masculinity. I personally am not really influenced by feelings of masculinity, so I don’t think it will influence the way I work with Dutch people in any shape or form.

Individualism

Having grown up in Switzerland, I think I can attest to this. Both countries are quite individualistic, and people in both countries tend to be quite focused on themselves and their families, rather than other people around them. But in general I also feel like people in the Netherlands are more extreme in that regard, wanting more individual freedoms, and wanting to provide less for the social good if not forced. This was quite well seen in the farmers protests a few years ago, where the farmers knowingly abused the lack of regulation for a few years, and then complained when regulations kicked back in. This will not influence teamwork or anything that much I think, the worst case scenario would be that I could get annoyed because someone wouldn’t want to do something for the team.

Ukrainian differences - Indulgence & Individualism - Serhii

Indulgence

From the table above, indulgence in Ukraine is low (14%), however in Netherlands, it is much higher (68%). I wasn’t surprised at all after I saw this comparison. Two days ago, I had interesting conversation with my roommates about our cultural differences, and one of my roommates (Also from Ukraine) mentioned “Most of population in Ukraine work to live and survive, but don’t live to enjoy and work”. Unfortunately, it is true, and I agreed with him. Of course, people with great jobs, who live in nice houses – they are enjoying their life, however another part of population who live in poor districts with bad salaries – do not enjoy their life, thus, they are working to survive. For instance, at work, employers do not care about employees and whether employees enjoy what they do or not. The most valuable things for most employers are productivity and profitability. I live in Netherlands for a month, and I noticed difference in quality of life between Ukraine and Netherlands. When I look at people outside, they seem to enjoy life and be happy to live. It was unusual for me because back in Ukraine, especially in poor districts, everyone was angry and depressed. Even in government facilities such as municipality or police, if you are late for appointment, no one will shout or be angry at you. I love people in Netherlands for this. Also, I think that in future this kindness and quality of life will have positive effect on my job and work in a team at company.

Individualism

Finally, in way of individualism, I totally agree with diagram above. It is true that in Netherlands people enjoy their life more than people in Ukraine. Also, people in Netherlands emphasize family life and traditions over job or work.
